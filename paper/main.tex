\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the staged development of an autonomous Ackermann-steered
scaled vehicle through five milestones in the MCTR~1002 Autonomous Systems
course. Milestone~1 establishes ROS\,2 Jazzy middleware on a Raspberry Pi~4
and validates the environment using the \texttt{ros2 doctor} diagnostic tool
across five health checks. Milestone~2 extends this foundation with a Gazebo
Harmonic simulation model, an Ackermann steering plugin, open-loop response
and keyboard teleoperation driving modes, and a physical actuator stack based
on a Raspberry Pi Pico microcontroller driving an L298N motor driver and MG995
steering servo. Milestone~3 introduces closed-loop PID speed control and
Stanley lateral control in simulation alongside a tuned hardware controller
with encoder feedback. Milestone~4 adds path planning across three structured
track environments---straight, racing with obstacles, and a city circuit---and
fuses encoder wheel speed with IMU gyroscope heading for dead-reckoning
odometry. Milestone~5 culminates in a discrete Kalman filter that optimally
combines odometry predictions with sensor measurements for robust state
estimation, enabling headless autonomous navigation on the physical platform.
We report complete system parameters, control gains, Kalman covariances, and
track test results for a repeatable open-source implementation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
ROS 2, Ackermann steering, autonomous vehicle, Kalman filter, path planning
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Autonomous wheeled vehicles require a tightly integrated chain of middleware,
dynamics modeling, control, planning, and state estimation before safe
navigation can be demonstrated. For an Ackermann-steered platform, this chain
must respect both the real-time communication constraints of the middleware
layer and the physical constraints of front-wheel steering geometry, making
full-stack integration a persistent challenge \cite{Jain2021}.
Recent advances in ROS\,2 and high-fidelity simulators such as Gazebo Harmonic
have lowered the barrier to entry for academic research on scaled platforms,
yet comprehensive open implementations that link hardware validation,
closed-loop control, path planning, and sensor fusion on a single low-cost
vehicle remain scarce in the published literature.

This paper documents the cumulative work of six undergraduate students in the
MCTR~1002 Autonomous Systems course at the German University in Cairo (GUC).
The project follows a staged development methodology in which each milestone
builds on the preceding one---from bare-metal ROS\,2 validation through
complete autonomous track navigation. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item ROS\,2 Jazzy validation on a Raspberry Pi~4 (Ubuntu Noble~24.04,
        ARM64) and a desktop running Gazebo Harmonic, with \texttt{ros2 doctor}
        diagnostics confirming all five health checks;
  \item a Gazebo Ackermann vehicle model bridged to ROS\,2 via
        \texttt{ros\_gz\_bridge}, supporting open-loop response testing and
        keyboard teleoperation;
  \item closed-loop PID speed control and Stanley lateral control, with gains
        tuned separately for simulation and for the scaled hardware platform;
  \item path planning across three structured track environments---straight,
        racing with obstacle avoidance, and a city circuit---using pre-parsed
        world file coordinates;
  \item dead-reckoning odometry fusing encoder wheel speed with IMU gyroscope
        heading on a Raspberry Pi Pico microcontroller;
  \item a discrete Kalman filter that optimally fuses odometry predictions
        with sensor measurements for headless autonomous navigation.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related work in robotics middleware, Ackermann control, and sensor
fusion. Section~\ref{sec:arch} presents the system architecture.
Sections~\ref{sec:m1} through~\ref{sec:ms5} detail each milestone's
deliverables. Section~\ref{sec:results} consolidates the experimental results,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} addresses design decisions and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Robotics Middleware and ROS\,2}

ROS\,2 uses a decentralized Data Distribution Service (DDS) communication
model that enables modular robotics systems where perception, planning,
control, and hardware interface nodes communicate via publish-subscribe topics
\cite{ROS2JazzyDocs}. The modular architecture supports staged development and
iterative testing \cite{Jain2021}, motivating our milestone-based methodology.
Gazebo Harmonic provides a high-fidelity simulation environment
\cite{GazeboHarmonicDocs} with plugin-based sensor and actuator models,
including an Ackermann steering plugin that models front-wheel steering
kinematics.

\subsection{Ackermann Steering and Vehicle Control}

The classic Ackermann constraint aligns all wheels with a common instantaneous
center of rotation. For a vehicle with track width $T$ and wheelbase $L$,
$\cot\delta_o - \cot\delta_i = T/L$ relates the outer and inner steering angles
\cite{Xu2023}. The kinematic bicycle model reduces this to a single effective
steering angle $\delta$, giving the yaw rate $\dot{\psi} = v \tan\delta / L$.
For lateral control, the Stanley controller \cite{Hoffmann2007} combines
heading error $\theta_e$ with a speed-adaptive cross-track correction,
$\delta = \theta_e + \arctan(k \cdot e_{\mathrm{ct}} / v)$, and has been
validated on full-scale autonomous vehicles. Proportional-integral-derivative
(PID) control remains the standard approach for longitudinal speed regulation
on both full-scale and scaled platforms owing to its simplicity and
effectiveness \cite{Wang2019}.

\subsection{Sensor Fusion for Localization}

Accurate state estimation is critical for autonomous navigation in the absence
of external localization infrastructure. Kalman-filter-based fusion of encoder
and inertial measurements has been applied to Ackermann mobile robots with
demonstrated improvements over open-loop odometry \cite{Peiris2024}. Zhang et
al.\ \cite{Zhang2022} propose electronic differential control for
multi-wheel Ackermann platforms. Georgiev \cite{Georgiev2025} surveys vehicle
localization through sensor fusion. Wu et al.\ \cite{Wu2022} demonstrate
MPC-based path tracking for distributed-drive electric vehicles. Despite this
body of work, a gap remains in the literature for an openly documented,
full-stack implementation that progresses stepwise from middleware validation
to sensor-fused autonomous navigation on a low-cost scaled Ackermann platform.

\label{sec:arch}

hardware---that share an identical command intent: a desired longitudinal speed
$v_{\mathrm{des}}$ and a desired steering angle $\delta_{\mathrm{des}}$.

Gazebo. On hardware, they are serialized as ASCII strings and sent over USB to
the Pico microcontroller. Figure~\ref{fig:arch_tikz} summarizes the five

The ROS\,2 package, named \texttt{Autonomous\_Systems\_Project\_Team\_23}, is
an \texttt{ament\_python} package containing launch files, Python nodes, and
Gazebo world assets. Each milestone adds functionality while preserving
earlier deliverables. The planning node generates reference trajectories and
publishes them to the speed and lateral controllers. Controller outputs are

vehicle through a serial interface. On the hardware path, the Pico firmware
reads encoder and IMU data at 50\,Hz, transmits odometry over serial back to
the Pi, and a discrete Kalman filter running on the Pi closes the loop by
providing corrected state estimates to the planner.

\subsection{Gazebo Vehicle Model and Bridge}

An Ackermann vehicle model (\texttt{prius\_team23}) was built with an SDF
description, mesh assets, and a Gazebo Ackermann steering plugin. The plugin
binds front steering joints and four wheel joints with a steering limit of
$0.5\,\mathrm{rad}$, wheelbase of $1.0\,\mathrm{m}$, wheel separation of
$1.25\,\mathrm{m}$, and wheel radius of $0.3\,\mathrm{m}$. The launch file
starts Gazebo Harmonic with \texttt{empty.sdf}, spawns the vehicle, starts
the ROS-Gazebo bridge, and launches the selected driving node. Four bridge
interfaces connect clock, command (\texttt{geometry\_msgs/Twist}), odometry
(\texttt{nav\_msgs/Odometry}), and joint states
(\texttt{sensor\_msgs/JointState}).

\subsection{Open-Loop Response and Teleoperation}

The open-loop response (OLR) node publishes a constant twist command at
10\,Hz and logs odometry and joint states. The yaw rate is computed from the
kinematic bicycle model:

\begin{equation}
  \dot{\psi} = \frac{v \tan \delta}{L}
  \label{eq:yawrate}
\end{equation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:m2_olr} shows the OLR demonstration. The teleoperation node
provides interactive control via keyboard: arrow keys adjust speed and
steering, space issues a stop, and \texttt{Q} triggers shutdown. Steering
commands are rate-limited before yaw-rate conversion. Fig.~\ref{fig:m2_teleop}
shows the teleoperation interface with real-time odometry feedback.

\section{Milestone 3: Closed-Loop Control}
\label{sec:ms3}
% ============================================================

Milestone~3 extends open-loop driving to closed-loop speed and lateral
tracking. The control laws are grounded in Ackermann geometry and implemented
as ROS\,2 nodes that can be deployed in simulation or on hardware.

\subsection{Ackermann Geometry}

The Ackermann condition constrains the steering angles of the front wheels to
intersect at a common instantaneous center of rotation:

\begin{equation}
  \cot\delta_{o} - \cot\delta_{i} = \frac{T}{L},
  \label{eq:ackermann}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_{o}$ and $\delta_{i}$ are the outer and inner steering angles,
$T$ is the track width, and $L$ is the wheelbase. For small angles,
\eqref{eq:ackermann} reduces to the kinematic bicycle model
\eqref{eq:yawrate}, which we use for both simulation ($L=1.0\,\mathrm{m}$)
and hardware ($L=0.255\,\mathrm{m}$).

\subsection{PID Speed Controller}

The longitudinal controller is a discrete PID regulator:

\begin{equation}
  u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_{0}^{t} e(\tau)\,d\tau + K_d \frac{d e(t)}{dt},
  \label{eq:pid}
\end{equation}

where $e(t) = v_{\mathrm{des}} - v_{\mathrm{meas}}$ is the speed error. On
the Pico, the loop runs at 50\,Hz with encoder-based speed feedback.
Table~\ref{tab:gains} lists the tuned gains across milestones.

\subsection{Stanley Lateral Controller}

The lateral controller implements the Stanley steering law
\cite{Hoffmann2007}:

\begin{equation}
  \delta(t) = \theta_e(t) + \arctan\!\left(\frac{k \cdot e_{\mathrm{ct}}(t)}{v(t)}\right),
  \label{eq:stanley}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_e$ is the heading error, $e_{\mathrm{ct}}$ is the cross-track
error, $v$ is the vehicle speed, and $k$ is a tunable gain. The Stanley law
provides direct heading-error feedback on straight segments and
speed-adaptive cross-track correction on curves, making it well suited to
our scaled platform.

Table~\ref{tab:gains} presents the initial gains used during MS3 simulation

both simulation and hardware after MS5 calibration.

\subsection{Dead-Reckoning Odometry}

The Pico firmware polls the quadrature encoder and MPU-6050 IMU at 50\,Hz.
Drive speed $v_d$ is computed from encoder ticks
($\mathrm{TICKS\_PER\_METER}=7000$), and yaw rate $\dot{\psi}$ is read
directly from the IMU gyroscope. The dead-reckoning state propagates as:

\begin{align}
  x_{k+1} &= x_k + v_d \cos\theta_k \,\Delta t, \label{eq:odom_x} \\
  y_{k+1} &= y_k + v_d \sin\theta_k \,\Delta t, \label{eq:odom_y} \\
  \theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k + \dot{\psi}_k \,\Delta t. \label{eq:odom_th}
\end{align}

The state vector $[v, x, y, \theta]$ is transmitted over serial to the
Raspberry Pi at 50\,Hz for logging and downstream processing.

\section{Milestone 5: Kalman Filter Localization}
\label{sec:ms5}
% ============================================================

Milestone~5 merges planning, control, and estimation into a headless
autonomous pipeline running locally on the Raspberry Pi. A discrete linear
Kalman filter fuses the kinematic predictions of
\eqref{eq:odom_x}--\eqref{eq:odom_th} with noisy sensor measurements.

\subsection{Filter Formulation}

The vehicle state vector is $\mathbf{x} = [x, y, \theta, v]^\top$. The
prediction step uses the kinematic model:

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} &= \mathbf{F}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1|k-1} + \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{u}_k, \label{eq:kf_pred} \\
  \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} &= \mathbf{F}_k \mathbf{P}_{k-1|k-1} \mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k, \label{eq:kf_pred_cov}
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{F}_k$ is the state transition matrix derived from
\eqref{eq:odom_x}--\eqref{eq:odom_th}, $\mathbf{B}_k$ maps the control input,
and $\mathbf{Q}_k$ is the process noise covariance. When a measurement
$\mathbf{z}_k$ arrives, the Kalman gain and update are:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{K}_k &= \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} \mathbf{H}_k^\top (\mathbf{H}_k \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} \mathbf{H}_k^\top + \mathbf{R}_k)^{-1}, \label{eq:kf_gain} \\
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k} &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} + \mathbf{K}_k (\mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}), \label{eq:kf_update} \\
  \mathbf{P}_{k|k} &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{H}_k) \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}, \label{eq:kf_update_cov}
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{H}_k$ is the observation matrix and $\mathbf{R}_k$ is the
measurement noise covariance.

\subsection{Filter Architecture}

Fig.~\ref{fig:kf_structure} illustrates the filter loop: the prediction step
propagates the state forward, the Kalman gain weights prediction versus
measurement, and the update step produces the corrected estimate that feeds
back into the next prediction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

The following points address key design decisions and limitations encountered
during development.

\begin{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Gain retuning between MS3 and MS5.} The initial controller
        gains were tuned on the Gazebo model, which uses a $1.0\,\mathrm{m}$
        wheelbase resembling a full-scale Prius. The physical vehicle has a
        $0.255\,\mathrm{m}$ wheelbase --- roughly $4\times$ shorter. Yaw rate
        scales inversely with wheelbase: $\dot{\psi} = v\tan\delta / L$, so the
        same steering input produces approximately $4\times$ larger yaw rate on
        the scaled platform. The initial gains ($K_p=1.2$, $K_i=0.35$) caused
        sustained oscillation on hardware. Reducing $K_p$ to $0.85$ and $K_i$
        to $0.10$ eliminated oscillation while maintaining steady-state
        accuracy, at the cost of a slightly longer rise time.

  \item \textbf{Stanley over Pure Pursuit.} The Stanley controller
        \eqref{eq:stanley} provides direct heading-error feedback through its
        $\theta_e$ term. On straight segments, Pure Pursuit relies entirely on
        a lookahead point and produces zero steering correction when the
        lookahead lies directly ahead, even if the vehicle has a heading error.
        Stanley's heading term stabilizes the vehicle on straights, while the
        arctan cross-track term provides asymptotic convergence on curves.
        This combination is better suited to the mixed straight--curved
        geometry of our three track types.

  \item \textbf{Kalman filter justification.} Encoder-only odometry
        accumulates heading error from gyroscope bias drift (approximately
        $0.04\;\mathrm{rad/s}$ standard deviation on the MPU-6050). The IMU
        provides accurate short-term heading but drives unbounded position
        drift over longer intervals through double integration. The Kalman
        filter combines the strengths of both sensors: encoder-based distance
        measurements constrain long-term position accuracy, while IMU yaw rate
        provides short-term heading corrections. The result is a fused estimate
        that outperforms either sensor alone.

  \item \textbf{Pico over Arduino Uno.} The Raspberry Pi Pico RP2040 offers
        hardware PWM on all pins, which is essential for generating the 50\,Hz
        servo signal without jitter. The Arduino Uno requires software PWM for
        an additional servo channel, introducing timing jitter that degraded
        steering precision. The Pico also provides dedicated I2C for the
        MPU-6050, interrupt-capable GPIO for the quadrature encoder, and a
        dual-core architecture that runs the 50\,Hz control loop on core~0 and
        serial communication on core~1, all on a \$4 board.

  \item \textbf{Limitation --- no dynamic obstacle detection.} The path
        planner uses pre-parsed obstacle coordinates from the world file.
        Dynamic obstacles or changes in the environment cannot be detected with
        the current proprioceptive-only sensor suite (encoder and IMU). A
        LiDAR or monocular camera would enable real-time perception and
        replanning.

  \item \textbf{Limitation --- time-based carriage.} The TT DC gear motor
        operates open-loop at the carriage level: position estimates rely on
        time integration of measured speed. Any unmodeled wheel slip, surface
        variation, or battery voltage drop produces unbounded carriage error.
        Adding encoder feedback to the drive motor (rather than measuring only
        wheel rotation) would close this loop.

  \item \textbf{Limitation --- Kalman linearization.} The linear state
        transition model \eqref{eq:odom_x}--\eqref{eq:odom_th} assumes
        constant velocity and yaw rate over each time step. During sharp $90^\circ$
        corners on the city track, this assumption introduces linearization
        error. An extended Kalman filter (EKF) or unscented Kalman filter
        (UKF) would better capture the nonlinear kinematics at the cost of
        additional computational complexity.

  \item \textbf{Limitation --- no SLAM.} The system assumes a known map and
        localizes through dead-reckoning with Kalman filter corrections.
        Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) would enable operation in
        unknown environments but requires exteroceptive sensors such as LiDAR
        or a depth camera not present on the current platform.

\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper presented the progressive development of an autonomous
Ackermann-steered vehicle through five milestones. Starting from bare-metal
ROS\,2 Jazzy validation on two platforms, we built a complete autonomy stack:
simulation driving modes in Gazebo Harmonic, closed-loop PID speed and Stanley
lateral control, path planning across three structured track environments,
dead-reckoning odometry with encoder and IMU fusion on a Raspberry Pi Pico,
and a discrete Kalman filter for robust state estimation on a Raspberry Pi~4.
The final system operates headlessly and successfully navigates straight,
obstacle-avoidance, and curved-circuit tracks. All source code, launch files,
and firmware are available as an open-source \texttt{ament\_python} package,
providing a repeatable foundation for further research on low-cost scaled
autonomous platforms.
