\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the staged development of an autonomous Ackermann-steered
scaled vehicle through five milestones in the MCTR~1002 Autonomous Systems
course. Milestone~1 established the ROS\,2 Jazzy middleware on a Raspberry
Pi~4 hardware computer and a desktop Gazebo Harmonic simulation environment.
Milestone~2 extended this foundation with an Ackermann vehicle model, a
ROS-Gazebo bridge, open-loop response and keyboard teleoperation driving
modes, and a physical Arduino-based actuator stack with DC motor and steering
servo. Milestone~3 added closed-loop speed and lateral control in simulation
alongside a tuned hardware controller with encoder feedback. Milestone~4
introduced path planning across three simulation tracks and integrated IMU and
encoder odometry on the physical platform. Milestone~5 culminated in a
discrete Kalman filter for state estimation, fusing noisy sensor measurements
to enable headless autonomous navigation through lane-following, obstacle
avoidance, and curved-track scenarios. We report complete system parameters,
control gains, Kalman covariances, and track test results, and we provide a
repeatable open-source ROS\,2 package and Arduino firmware.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
ROS 2, Ackermann steering, autonomous vehicle, Kalman filter, path planning
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Autonomous wheeled vehicles require a chain of computation, middleware,
vehicle dynamics, perception, planning, and control before safe autonomous
navigation can be demonstrated. For an Ackermann-steered platform, this chain
must respect both the software communication model and the physical
constraints of front-wheel steering geometry, making integration a
recognized challenge \cite{Jain2021}.

This paper documents the cumulative work of six undergraduate students in the
MCTR~1002 Autonomous Systems course at the German University in Cairo (GUC).
The project follows a staged development methodology---from bare-metal ROS\,2
validation through complete autonomous track navigation---with each milestone
building on the preceding one. Milestone~1 validated the ROS\,2 Jazzy
environment on two platforms. Milestone~2 added vehicle-level driving modes
and actuator testing. Milestone~3 introduced closed-loop control. Milestone~4
integrated path planning and sensor odometry. Milestone~5 realized full system
integration with Kalman-filtered localization.

Despite the breadth of prior work on individual components, comprehensive
open implementations linking ROS\,2 hardware validation, closed-loop control,
path planning, and sensor fusion on a low-cost scaled platform remain scarce
in the published literature. Our contributions are therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item ROS\,2 Jazzy validation on a Raspberry Pi~4 (Ubuntu Noble~24.04, ARM64)
        and an AMD Ryzen~9 laptop running Gazebo Harmonic;
  \item an Ackermann vehicle model spawned in Gazebo and bridged to ROS\,2 via
        \texttt{ros\_gz\_bridge};
  \item open-loop response and keyboard teleoperation ROS\,2 Python nodes with
        logged odometry and joint-state feedback;
  \item closed-loop speed and lateral controllers with tunable PID and Stanley
        gains, deployed in both simulation and hardware;
  \item a tuned Arduino firmware that parses serial commands, performs PID speed
        regulation with encoder feedback, and controls the steering servo;
  \item a planning node that generates velocity profiles and lane-change
        maneuvers across three Gazebo track environments (Empty, Racing, City);
  \item integration of IMU and quadrature encoder measurements into a dead-reckoning
        odometry pipeline, transmitted over serial at 115\,200 baud;
  \item a discrete Kalman filter that fuses noisy odometry with kinematic
        predictions, enabling headless autonomous track navigation on the
        physical vehicle.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews the technical background in robotics middleware, Ackermann geometry,
localization, and control. Section~\ref{sec:arch} presents the system
architecture across all milestones. Sections~\ref{sec:m1} through~\ref{sec:ms5}
detail each milestone's deliverables in chronological order.
Section~\ref{sec:results} consolidates the achieved results, and
Section~\ref{sec:future} discusses limitations and future extensions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Robotics Middleware}
ROS\,2 uses a decentralized Data Distribution Service (DDS) communication
model that enables modular robotics systems where perception, planning,
control, and hardware-interface nodes communicate via publish-subscribe
topics \cite{ROS2JazzyDocs}. Recent surveys of autonomous vehicle
architectures identify this modular pattern as essential for staged
development and iterative testing \cite{Jain2021}, motivating our
milestone-by-milestone approach.

\subsection{Ackermann Steering Geometry}
The classic Ackermann constraint aligns all wheels with a common instantaneous
center of rotation. The geometric relation is
\begin{equation}
  \cot\delta_{o} - \cot\delta_{i} = \frac{T}{L},
  \label{eq:ackermann}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:ackermann}, $\delta_{o}$ and $\delta_{i}$ are the outer and inner steering angles,
$T$ is the track width, and $L$ is the wheelbase \cite{Xu2023}. Our
simulation model uses a Gazebo Ackermann steering plugin with a wheelbase of
$1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ in simulation and $0.255\,\mathrm{m}$ on the scaled hardware
platform.

\subsection{Localization and Sensor Fusion}
Accurate state estimation is critical for autonomous navigation.
Kalman-filter-based fusion of encoder and inertial measurements has been
applied to Ackermann mobile robots with demonstrated improvements over
open-loop odometry \cite{Peiris2024}. Vehicle localization through sensor
fusion for autonomous driving continues to be an active research area, with
LiDAR, camera, and IMU configurations achieving lane-level accuracy
\cite{Georgiev2025}.

\subsection{Path Planning and Control}
Trajectory planning and tracking control are tightly coupled in autonomous
driving stacks. Real-time dynamic planning \cite{Wang2019}, MPC-based
obstacle avoidance \cite{Wu2022}, and electronic differential control for
multi-wheel Ackermann platforms \cite{Zhang2022} have been proposed. For
lateral tracking, the Stanley controller \cite{Hoffmann2007} combines heading
error and cross-track error with a speed-adaptive gain, making it well suited
to our scaled Ackermann platform.

The ROS\,2 package, named \texttt{Autonomous\_Systems\_Project\_Team\_23}, is
an \texttt{ament\_python} package. Each milestone adds launch files, node
scripts, and Gazebo world assets while preserving the earlier functionality.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch_tikz} shows the complete data flow. The planning node

serial interface. On the hardware path, the Pico firmware reads encoder and
IMU data, transmits odometry over serial back to the Pi, and a Kalman filter
closes the loop by providing corrected state estimates to the planner.

\begin{equation}
  \dot{\psi} = \frac{v \tan \delta}{L},

\end{equation}

\section{Milestone 2: Hardware Actuation}
\label{sec:hwms2}
% ============================================================

The hardware deliverable tests the physical vehicle's drive and steering
actuators. The Arduino sketch configures an H-bridge motor driver (L298N),
a steering servo (MG995), and quadrature encoder inputs.

\subsection{Serial Command Interface}

Commands are sent over USB serial at 115\,200\,baud in the form
\texttt{SPD:<speed>,STR:<steering>}, where speed is in~$\mathrm{m/s}$ and
steering is in~$\mathrm{rad}$. A 500\,ms timeout forces zero speed and clears
the integral term for safety.

\subsection{Motor and Steering Control}

The controller estimates wheel speed from encoder ticks using
$\mathrm{TICKS\_PER\_METER}=7000$ and the configured wheel diameter. A PID
controller computes the speed error and constrains the PWM output to the
usable range ($\mathrm{DRIVE\_PWM}=150$). Motor direction is set by the sign
of the target speed. Steering commands are mapped to the servo range
$[50, 130]$ around a $90^\circ$ center.

\subsection{PID Speed Control Equation}

The PID speed controller output is:
\begin{equation}
  u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_{0}^{t} e(\tau)\,d\tau + K_d \frac{d e(t)}{dt},
  \label{eq:pid}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:pid}, $e(t) = v_{\mathrm{des}} - v_{\mathrm{meas}}$ is the speed error.
Table~\ref{tab:gains} lists the tuned gains. Fig.~\ref{fig:m2_hw}
demonstrates the physical actuator test on the assembled scaled vehicle.

\section{Milestone 3: Closed-Loop Control}
\label{sec:ms3}
% ============================================================

Milestone~3 extends open-loop driving to closed-loop speed and lateral
tracking. A speed control node publishes a filtered speed command with
acceleration limiting. The lateral controller subscribes to odometry and the
speed command, applies a proportional-derivative lateral controller with
heading feedback, and publishes the final \texttt{Twist} to Gazebo.

\subsection{Stanley Lateral Controller}

The lateral controller implements the Stanley steering law \cite{Hoffmann2007}:
\begin{equation}
  \delta(t) = \theta_e(t) + \arctan\!\left(\frac{k \cdot e_{\mathrm{ct}}(t)}{v(t)}\right),
  \label{eq:stanley}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:stanley}, $\theta_e$ is the heading error, $e_{\mathrm{ct}}$ is the cross-track
error, $v$ is the vehicle speed, and $k$ is the tunable gain.

\subsection{Dead-Reckoning Odometry}

The hardware platform computes dead-reckoning position from encoder and IMU
measurements. At each time step $\Delta t$, the state propagates as:
\begin{align}
  x_{k+1} &= x_k + v_d \cos\theta_k \,\Delta t, \label{eq:odom_x} \\
  y_{k+1} &= y_k + v_d \sin\theta_k \,\Delta t, \label{eq:odom_y} \\
  \theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k + \dot{\psi}_k \,\Delta t, \label{eq:odom_th}
\end{align}
where $v_d$ is the measured drive speed and $\dot{\psi}_k$ is the yaw rate
from \eqref{eq:yawrate} or from the IMU gyroscope.

The launch files expose all gain parameters for speed and lateral tuning. On
hardware, the Arduino controller was retuned with measured encoder counts at
a 50\,Hz control loop rate. Feedforward scaling and a 2\,s serial timeout were
added for safety.

\section{Milestone 5: Kalman Filter Localization and Full Integration}
\label{sec:ms5}
% ============================================================

Milestone~5 represents the culmination of the project: merging planning,
control, and perception into a headless autonomous execution pipeline
running locally on the Raspberry Pi.

\subsection{Kalman Filter State Estimation}

Real-world sensors produce noisy measurements. We implemented a discrete
linear Kalman filter to fuse odometry predictions with sensor observations.
The vehicle state vector is $\mathbf{x} = [x, y, \theta, v]^\top$.

\subsubsection{Prediction Step}

The state and covariance are predicted using the kinematic model:
\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} &= \mathbf{F}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1|k-1} + \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{u}_k, \label{eq:kf_pred} \\
  \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} &= \mathbf{F}_k \mathbf{P}_{k-1|k-1} \mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k, \label{eq:kf_cov}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{F}_k$ is the state transition matrix derived from
\eqref{eq:odom_x}--\eqref{eq:odom_th}, $\mathbf{B}_k$ maps the control input,
and $\mathbf{Q}_k$ is the process noise covariance matrix.

\subsubsection{Update Step}

When a measurement $\mathbf{z}_k$ arrives, the filter updates its estimate:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{K}_k &= \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} \mathbf{H}_k^\top (\mathbf{H}_k \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} \mathbf{H}_k^\top + \mathbf{R}_k)^{-1}, \label{eq:kf_gain} \\
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k} &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} + \mathbf{K}_k (\mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}), \label{eq:kf_update} \\
  \mathbf{P}_{k|k} &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{H}_k) \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}, \label{eq:kf_cov_update}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{K}_k$ is the Kalman gain, $\mathbf{H}_k$ is the observation
matrix, and $\mathbf{R}_k$ is the measurement noise covariance.

The vehicle successfully navigated Track~1 with accurate speed profile
tracking and zero lateral error. On Track~2, the planner triggered
lane-change maneuvers to avoid all three static obstacles before returning

using IMU heading feedback. The Kalman filter reduced the root-mean-square

and high-level decision making (Raspberry Pi ROS\,2 nodes) proved robust:
the vehicle can be developed, tuned, and tested in simulation, then deployed
to hardware with only the serial bridge layer changing.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
% ============================================================

While the system demonstrated end-to-end autonomous navigation using
predefined map knowledge and odometry-based localization, several
capabilities remain for future development:

\begin{itemize}
  \item integrate LiDAR and camera perception for dynamic obstacle detection
        and visual lane tracking, replacing predefined obstacle coordinates;
  \item transition from deterministic lane-change rules to dynamic
        Frenet-frame planning and model predictive control (MPC);
  \item implement simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) to operate in
        unmapped environments;
  \item extend the linear Kalman filter to an extended Kalman filter (EKF)
        or unscented Kalman filter (UKF) to better handle the nonlinear
        Ackermann kinematics at higher speeds;
  \item add collision monitoring and emergency stop behavior as a ROS\,2
        lifecycle node for safety-critical deployment.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper presented the progressive development of an autonomous
Ackermann-steered vehicle through five milestones. Starting from bare-metal
ROS\,2 Jazzy validation on two platforms, we built a complete autonomy stack:
simulation driving modes, closed-loop PID and Stanley control, path planning
across structured tracks, dead-reckoning odometry with IMU and encoder fusion,
and a discrete Kalman filter for robust state estimation. The final system
operates headlessly on a Raspberry Pi~4 with a Pico-based actuator interface
and successfully navigates three distinct track configurations. All source
code, launch files, and firmware are available as an open-source
\texttt{ament\_python} package, providing a repeatable foundation for further
research on low-cost scaled autonomous platforms.
